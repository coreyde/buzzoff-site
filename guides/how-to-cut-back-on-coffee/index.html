How to cut back on coffee without quitting

By Corey De Ponte, founder of Buzz Off · Last reviewed: 26 November 2026

Short answer

Lower your caffeine in small steps rather than all at once: in one experiment, people used to 300 mg a day only showed withdrawal once their dose dropped to 100 mg a day or less.⁠[1] Start from what you have now, then trim a little each week with smaller cups, half-caf, decaf or tea.

Start with what you have now

Cups vary, so a count of “cups” can hide a lot. EFSA gives about 90 mg for a 200 ml cup of filter coffee and about 80 mg for a 60 ml espresso.⁠[2] Coffee-shop cups can be much more: one study found the same drink bought from the same outlet on six days in a row ranged from 259 to 564 mg.⁠[3] Add up a typical day with the caffeine calculator so you know your starting point.

Ways to cut back

  • Go smaller. A smaller cup or a single shot instead of a double keeps the ritual with less caffeine.
  • Try half-caf. Mixing regular and decaf lands somewhere in between, and you can shift the mix over time.
  • Make later cups decaf. Decaf isn’t caffeine-free, but the FDA says it typically has 2 to 15 mg in an 8-fluid-ounce cup.⁠[4] More in caffeine in decaf.
  • Swap a cup for tea. EFSA gives about 50 mg for a 220 ml cup of black tea, against about 90 mg for a 200 ml filter coffee.⁠[2] See caffeine in tea.
  • Move your last cup earlier. The authors of a sleep study recommend avoiding substantial caffeine for at least 6 hours before bedtime,⁠[5] so the afternoon cup is often a good one to change first.

How fast should you cut back?

Slowly enough that you can keep going. The FDA says that if you’re used to caffeine every day and want to cut back, it’s best to do so gradually.⁠[4] In the experiment above, symptoms only appeared once daily caffeine dropped a long way, to 100 mg or less,⁠[1] which suggests smaller steps may be gentler for many people.

Buzz Off’s Gradual plan, which the taper planner follows, runs for six weeks from your usual daily amount: week 1 is for tracking at your usual amount, then each week’s target is about 15% lower than the week before, and it never goes below 25 mg. That’s the app’s own rule, not medical guidance. Try it with your own number in the taper planner.

How much is a reasonable amount?

That’s up to you and your doctor, not a target. For context, the FDA has cited 400 mg a day, about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee, as an amount not generally associated with negative effects for most adults.⁠[4] EFSA says up to 200 mg a day doesn’t raise safety concerns for the foetus in pregnancy.⁠[2] The FDA adds that people vary widely in how sensitive they are to caffeine.⁠[4] If you’d rather stop altogether, see how to quit caffeine.

Questions

How can I cut down on coffee without withdrawal?

Cut back in small steps. In one experiment, people used to 300 mg a day only showed withdrawal once their dose dropped to 100 mg a day or less,⁠[1] and the FDA says it’s best to cut back gradually.⁠[4]

Does switching to decaf help?

It lowers your caffeine a lot, but not to zero: the FDA says decaf typically has 2 to 15 mg in an 8-fluid-ounce cup.⁠[4]

How many cups of coffee a day is too much?

For most adults, the FDA has cited 400 mg a day, about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee, as an amount not generally associated with negative effects.⁠[4] People vary, so talk to your doctor if you’re unsure.

Is tea a good way to cut back on coffee?

It can be. EFSA gives about 50 mg for a 220 ml cup of black tea, against about 90 mg for a 200 ml cup of filter coffee.⁠[2]
