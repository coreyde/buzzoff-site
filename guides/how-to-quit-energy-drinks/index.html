How to quit energy drinks

By Corey De Ponte, founder of Buzz Off · Last reviewed: 26 November 2026

Short answer

You can stop energy drinks all at once or cut back a can at a time; the FDA says that if you’re used to caffeine every day, it’s best to cut back gradually.[1] Either way, withdrawal symptoms such as headache and tiredness typically start 12–24 hours after your last caffeine and last 2–9 days.[2]

How much caffeine are you having?

Start with the cans you actually drink, because they vary a lot. The FDA says energy drinks generally have 54 to 328 mg of caffeine per 16 fluid ounces, with a few brands containing 41 to 246 mg per 12 fluid ounces.[1] EFSA gives about 80 mg for a standard 250 ml can.[3]

The label is your best guide. The FDA says most energy drinks in the U.S. list the total caffeine from all sources, including ingredients like guarana.[1] In the EU, drinks with more than 150 mg of caffeine per litre (other than coffee and tea) must say “High caffeine content” and give the caffeine per 100 ml.[4] Add up a normal day with the caffeine calculator.

Cold turkey or cutting back?

There’s no single right answer. Stopping all at once gets withdrawal over sooner: symptoms typically peak at 20–51 hours and last 2–9 days.[2] Cutting back spreads the change out. In one experiment, people used to 300 mg a day only showed withdrawal once their dose dropped to 100 mg a day or less.[5] Our guide on how to quit caffeine covers both in more detail.

How to cut back on energy drinks

  • Know your number. Read the caffeine on each can and add up a typical day.
  • Drop one can, or part of one, at a time. Smaller steps may be easier to stick with. Buzz Off’s Gradual plan, which the taper planner follows, runs for six weeks from your usual daily amount: week 1 is for tracking at your usual amount, then each week’s target is about 15% lower than the week before, and it never goes below 25 mg. That’s the app’s own rule, not medical guidance. You can build your own plan with the taper planner.
  • Swap the habit, not just the drink. If you reach for a can at the same time every day, have something else ready then: water, a herbal tea (USDA lists 0 mg of caffeine for brewed chamomile[6]) or a lower-caffeine drink while you step down. See coffee swaps for ideas.

What to expect when you stop

The withdrawal symptoms a major review confirmed include headache, fatigue, low energy, drowsiness, low mood, difficulty concentrating and irritability.[2] Symptoms generally got more frequent or more severe as the usual daily dose went up.[2] The FDA notes that caffeine withdrawal isn’t considered dangerous but can be unpleasant.[1] Our caffeine withdrawal guide has the full timeline, and the withdrawal headache guide covers what may help.

Signs you may be having too much

The FDA lists increased heart rate, heart palpitations, high blood pressure, sleep problems, anxiety, jitters, upset stomach, nausea and headache as signs of too much caffeine, and suggests talking with your health care professional if you have any of them.[1] It also notes that medical experts advise against energy drinks for children and teens.[1]

Questions

How much caffeine is in an energy drink?

The FDA says energy drinks generally have 54 to 328 mg of caffeine per 16 fluid ounces.[1] EFSA gives about 80 mg for a standard 250 ml can.[3] Check the label for your brand.

Is it better to quit energy drinks cold turkey?

Either can work. The FDA says it’s best to cut back gradually if you’re used to caffeine every day.[1] Stopping at once gets withdrawal over sooner; it typically lasts 2–9 days.[2]

How long does energy drink withdrawal last?

Caffeine withdrawal typically starts 12–24 hours after your last caffeine, peaks at 20–51 hours and lasts 2–9 days.[2]

What can I drink instead of an energy drink?

Water or a herbal tea if you want no caffeine (USDA lists 0 mg for brewed chamomile[6]), or a lower-caffeine drink while you cut back.
