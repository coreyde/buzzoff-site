How to quit caffeine: cold turkey or gradually

By Corey De Ponte, founder of Buzz Off · Last reviewed: 26 November 2026

Short answer

You can stop all at once (cold turkey) or cut back a little at a time, and the best way is the one you’ll stick with. Withdrawal symptoms typically start 12–24 hours after your last caffeine and last 2–9 days,⁠[1] and in one experiment, people used to 300 mg a day only showed withdrawal once their dose dropped to 100 mg a day or less.⁠[2]

Cold turkey or cutting back: how to choose

Neither way is right for everyone. It helps to know what each one asks of you.

  • Cold turkey gives you one clear start date. Any withdrawal comes at once, usually in the first days. Many people like the clean break and counting their caffeine-free days.
  • Cutting back gradually spreads the change out. You lower your daily amount step by step, so each change is smaller.

Research supports giving the gradual route a fair look. In one controlled experiment, people used to 300 mg of caffeine a day were switched to lower doses, and withdrawal only showed up once the dose fell to 100 mg a day or less.⁠[2] The same research found withdrawal got stronger as the usual daily dose went up.

What to expect in the first days

A review of 66 studies found that withdrawal symptoms typically start 12–24 hours after your last caffeine, peak at 20–51 hours, and last 2–9 days.⁠[1] The symptoms it confirmed include headache, fatigue, low energy, drowsiness, low mood, difficulty concentrating and irritability. Our guide to caffeine withdrawal covers each one.

Withdrawal can happen even if you don’t have much caffeine: the same review found symptoms after stopping doses as low as 100 mg a day.⁠[1]

How to quit cold turkey

  • Pick a start day with a lighter schedule, so the first two days are easier to get through.
  • Keep the ritual and change the drink: a warm mug of decaf or herbal tea at your usual coffee time.
  • Plan for the dip. Many people feel more tired than usual at first, so go easy on yourself. See tiredness after quitting caffeine.
  • Note how you feel each day. Seeing symptoms ease over a few days can be encouraging.

How to cut back gradually

  • Start by knowing your number. Our guide to caffeine in drinks helps you add up a typical day.
  • Lower your daily amount a little at a time, and hold each step for about a week before the next.
  • Swap one drink at a time: a decaf for your afternoon coffee, a smaller size, or half-caf. More in how to cut back on coffee.
  • Log what you have. It’s easy to lose track of the odd tea or cola.

Watch for hidden caffeine

Coffee isn’t the only source. Tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate all contain caffeine, and even decaf has a little. EFSA gives about 50 mg for a 220 ml cup of black tea and about 40 mg for a 355 ml can of cola.⁠[3] If energy drinks are your main source, see how to quit energy drinks.

Is my daily amount a lot?

For most adults, the FDA has cited 400 mg a day, about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee, as an amount not generally associated with negative effects.⁠[4] EFSA says up to 200 mg a day doesn’t raise safety concerns for the foetus in pregnancy.⁠[3] You don’t have to be over any line to want to cut back. If caffeine leaves you jittery or anxious, see caffeine and anxiety.

Questions

Is it better to quit caffeine cold turkey or gradually?

There’s no single right answer. Cold turkey gets withdrawal over with sooner; withdrawal typically lasts 2–9 days.⁠[1] Cutting back spreads the change out, and in one experiment withdrawal only appeared once people used to 300 mg a day dropped to 100 mg a day or less.⁠[2]

How long does it take to quit caffeine?

Withdrawal symptoms typically start 12–24 hours after your last caffeine and last 2–9 days.⁠[1] Building the new habit can take longer, and that varies from person to person.

How much caffeine is too much?

For most adults, the FDA has cited 400 mg a day as an amount not generally associated with negative effects.⁠[4] If you’re pregnant, EFSA’s figure is up to 200 mg a day.⁠[3] Talk to your doctor if you’re unsure.

What if I have a coffee after quitting?

In one set of experiments, people given 300 mg of caffeine a day showed withdrawal when it stopped after as little as 3 days, and withdrawal was somewhat more severe after 7 or 14 days of caffeine.⁠[2]

Should I talk to a doctor before quitting caffeine?

The FDA recommends talking to your health care provider about limiting caffeine if you’re concerned about a condition or medication, or if you’re pregnant, trying to become pregnant or breastfeeding.⁠[4]
