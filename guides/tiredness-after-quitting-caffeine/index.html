How long does tiredness last after quitting caffeine?

By Corey De Ponte, founder of Buzz Off · Last reviewed: 26 November 2026

Short answer

Tiredness is one of the confirmed symptoms of caffeine withdrawal: it typically starts 12–24 hours after your last caffeine, peaks at 20–51 hours, and withdrawal lasts 2–9 days, according to a review of 66 studies.[1] If you’re still tired after a few weeks and aren’t sure why, the NHS suggests seeing a GP.[2]

Why does quitting caffeine make you tired?

Fatigue, low energy, decreased alertness and drowsiness are all among the withdrawal symptoms a major review confirmed.[1] A study of 379 people found that in regular caffeine users, going without caffeine lowered alertness, and having caffeine mostly brought alertness back to normal rather than above it.[3]

A review of the research came to a similar view: the lift many people feel from their usual coffee may be almost entirely the reversal of withdrawal that builds up between doses.[4] So part of the tiredness you notice after quitting may be your body adjusting to working without it.

How long it lasts2–9 days[1]

These are typical ranges from research, not a schedule, and everyone’s different. The review also found that symptoms generally got more frequent or more severe as the usual daily dose went up,[1] so the more caffeine you were used to, the more you may notice it. What may help with the tiredness?

  • Cut back step by step instead. The FDA says that if you’re used to caffeine every day and want to cut back, it’s best to do so gradually.[5] In one experiment, people used to 300 mg a day only showed withdrawal once their dose dropped to 100 mg a day or less.[6] The taper planner builds a week-by-week plan from your own starting point.
  • Protect your sleep. The NHS suggests sticking to the same sleep times and aiming for 6 to 9 hours of sleep.[2]
  • Try a short nap. In a study of 24 young adults who had slept about 5 hours, a 10-minute afternoon nap improved sleepiness, fatigue and performance straight away, while a 30-minute nap left people groggy at first.[7]
  • Move a little. In a small study of 18 young women short on sleep, 10 minutes of stair walking left them feeling more energetic than a 50 mg caffeine capsule, though the effect was short-lived.[8] More ideas are in our guide to the afternoon slump without coffee.
  • Plan a lighter few days. If you’re quitting all at once, many people find it easier to start before a quieter stretch, since symptoms typically peak in the first couple of days.[1]

When could tiredness be something else?

Caffeine withdrawal typically passes within days.[1] The NHS says to see a GP if you’ve been feeling tired for a few weeks and you’re not sure why, if tiredness affects your daily life, or if you feel tired and have other symptoms such as weight loss or mood changes.[2] Common causes of tiredness it lists include not getting enough sleep, stress and some illnesses and medicines.[2]

Questions

How long does tiredness last after quitting caffeine?

Caffeine withdrawal, which includes tiredness, typically starts 12–24 hours after your last caffeine and lasts 2–9 days.[1] Everyone’s different, and the more caffeine you were used to, the more you may notice it.

Why am I so tired after quitting coffee?

Fatigue and drowsiness are confirmed caffeine withdrawal symptoms.[1] Research suggests regular caffeine mostly brings alertness back to normal after the dip between doses, so going without can feel flat at first.[3]

Is it better to cut back slowly to avoid tiredness?

Many people find it easier. The FDA says it’s best to cut back gradually if you’re used to caffeine every day,[5] and in one experiment withdrawal only appeared once people used to 300 mg a day dropped to 100 mg a day or less.[6]

When should I see a doctor about tiredness?

The NHS suggests seeing a GP if you’ve been tired for a few weeks and aren’t sure why, if it affects your daily life, or if you have other symptoms such as weight loss or mood changes.[2]
