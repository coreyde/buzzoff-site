Caffeine withdrawal: symptoms and how long it lasts

By Corey De Ponte, founder of Buzz Off · Last reviewed: 26 November 2026

Short answer

Caffeine withdrawal symptoms typically start 12–24 hours after your last caffeine, peak at 20–51 hours and last 2–9 days, according to a review of 66 studies (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004). Symptoms the review confirmed include headache, fatigue, low energy, drowsiness, low mood, difficulty concentrating and irritability.

Symptoms

Of 49 symptom types reported in studies, these 10 met the review’s criteria for being real withdrawal effects (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004):

  • headache
  • fatigue
  • decreased energy or activeness
  • decreased alertness
  • drowsiness
  • decreased contentedness
  • depressed mood
  • difficulty concentrating
  • irritability
  • feeling foggy or not clear-headed

The review judged flu-like symptoms, nausea or vomiting, and muscle pain or stiffness as likely withdrawal symptoms too. In the experimental studies it reviewed, the incidence of headache was 50%, and the incidence of clinically significant distress or functional impairment was 13% (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004). So many people get some symptoms, and fewer find them hard to function with.

These are typical ranges from research, not a schedule. Everyone’s different.

Can a small amount of caffeine cause withdrawal?

Yes. The review found that stopping doses as low as 100 mg a day produced symptoms, and that symptoms generally got more frequent or more severe as the usual daily dose went up (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004).

In a double-blind study of 62 adults whose usual intake averaged 235 mg a day (the equivalent of 2.5 cups of coffee), 52% had a moderate or severe headache when their caffeine was secretly swapped for a placebo (Silverman et al., 1992). Another set of experiments found withdrawal after as little as 3 days of regular caffeine (Evans & Griffiths, 1999).

Why you may feel tired

A study of 379 people found that in regular caffeine users, going without caffeine lowered alertness, and having caffeine mostly brought alertness back to normal rather than above it (Rogers et al., 2010). That may be part of why the first days without caffeine can feel flat.

What may help

  • Cut back step by step. In one experiment, people used to 300 mg a day only showed withdrawal once their dose dropped to 100 mg a day or less (Evans & Griffiths, 1999). See how to quit caffeine.
  • Plan for the first couple of days. Symptoms typically peak at 20–51 hours (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004), so a lighter schedule then may help.
  • Look after the basics. Water, regular meals and sleep won’t stop withdrawal, but many people find they make the days easier.
  • Keep a warm drink in your routine with a caffeine-free swap.

Questions

How long does caffeine withdrawal last?

Typically 2–9 days, with symptoms starting 12–24 hours after your last caffeine and peaking at 20–51 hours (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004).

What are the first signs of caffeine withdrawal?

Headache and tiredness are among the symptoms the research confirmed, along with low energy, drowsiness, low mood, difficulty concentrating and irritability (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004).

Can I get caffeine withdrawal from just one coffee a day?

It’s possible. A review found symptoms after stopping doses as low as 100 mg a day (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004). That’s around a cup of coffee, depending on the cup: EFSA gives about 90 mg for a 200 ml filter coffee (EFSA).

Is caffeine withdrawal a real condition?

Yes. A 2004 review concluded the syndrome “has been well characterized” and that the evidence was sufficient to include caffeine withdrawal as a disorder in the DSM (Juliano & Griffiths, 2004).
